# TikTok and Snapchat: The Settings That Matter (and How to Set Them Together)

## Learning objective

After reading this article, you will be able to run five worksites with a young person that change how they use TikTok and Snapchat: locking down visibility, mastering location, understanding the mechanics of metrics and streaks, setting a sustainable screen-time frame, and turning on both platforms' official family tools.

---

## A situation to know about

They open TikTok to watch one video. Forty minutes later, they're still scrolling. In between, they've checked notifications three times, posted something they weren't sure about "because it had been a while", and left a comment they already regret.

None of that was a conscious choice — and that's by design. TikTok and Snapchat are built by entire teams whose job is to hold attention as long as possible. The features that make these platforms enjoyable — the algorithm, the streaks, the notifications, the counters — are exactly the ones that make them hard to use in a balanced way.

This article doesn't argue for a ban: it lays out the five worksites, setting by setting, so a young person uses these platforms on their terms rather than the platforms'. It's the platform-by-platform continuation of [the 5 practices to build together](/en/articles/youth-online-5-good-practices).

---

## A prerequisite that changes everything: the age declared at sign-up

Contrary to popular belief, both platforms apply default protections to minors' accounts: on TikTok, accounts of under-16s are **private by default**, with no direct messages, and a **60-minute daily limit** applies by default to under-18s; on Snapchat, contact is limited to friends by default for everyone.

These protections rest on one thing only: **the date of birth declared at sign-up.** A teenager who declared themselves an adult inherits adult defaults — public account, open messages, no limit. It's the first thing to check together, before everything else: if the account's age is wrong, the clean fix is to correct it or recreate the account, not to pile settings on top.

## The 5 worksites

### Worksite 1 — Lock down visibility

Even with the right date, an account deserves a settings pass done side by side. Interfaces change regularly — search for the names of the settings rather than memorizing menu paths.

**On TikTok** (Settings → Privacy): **private** account; direct messages limited to **friends**; "**Suggest your account to others**" turned off; liked-videos visibility set to only me.

**On Snapchat** (Settings, Privacy section): "Who can contact me" and "View my story" set to **My Friends**; "**See my location**" — see worksite 2; appearance in **Quick Add** (friend suggestions) turned off if the young person doesn't want to be findable by strangers.

Five minutes per platform, repeated every few months — updates sometimes reset or move options.

---

### Worksite 2 — Location: Snap Map and backgrounds

Location leaks through two very different channels.

**The technical channel — Snap Map:** Snapchat can display the young person's position continuously on a map visible to their friends — and "their friends", after years of adding, often includes people they barely know. The setting: **Ghost Mode** on, or position visible to a restricted list chosen together.

**The human channel — the content itself:** a TikTok video or a story reveals the school through a uniform, the street through a window, the routine through posting times. **The 20-second scan before posting:** watch the video or photo through the eyes of someone who doesn't know you — what can they deduce? Backgrounds, location tags, captions. And one reality to pass on about Snapchat: the screenshot notification exists, but it's easily bypassed — **every snap should be treated as recordable and re-shareable.**

---

### Worksite 3 — Understand the mechanics: metrics and streaks

Views, likes, followers, story views — and Snapchat's **streaks**: the counter of consecutive days exchanging with a contact, designed to create a daily obligation. Missing a day "breaks the streak", and that pressure is real among teenagers — some hand their login to a friend to keep streaks alive during holidays, which is exactly the kind of account sharing to avoid.

**What helps, on the adult's side:** naming the mechanic rather than moralizing it. These numbers are designed by the platform to trigger emotional responses — the satisfaction of a post that performs, the anxiety of one that doesn't, the compulsion to post to "keep it up". The question to pass on: *am I posting because I have something to share, or because the counter expects it?* A young person who understands they're the object of a design no longer experiences it the same way.

---

### Worksite 4 — Screen time: the technique AND the negotiation

TikTok's algorithm is exceptionally effective: the forty-minute sessions that feel like five are not an accident — they're the objective. Scrolling has no natural stopping point, so one has to be installed.

**The technique:** on TikTok, screen-time management is built in (Settings → Screen time) — the minors' default limit is 60 minutes, a good anchor; on Snapchat, use the phone's tools (**Screen Time** on iPhone, **Digital Wellbeing** on Android). And the highest-yield change on both platforms: **turn off push notifications.** Check the app when you choose to, not when it calls.

**The negotiation:** a unilaterally imposed limit gets bypassed; a negotiated limit holds. Set the frame together — the number, the screen-free moments (meals, evening), and what happens if it overruns — applying the track's golden rule: the frame is discussed, not sprung as a surprise.

---

### Worksite 5 — Report, block, escalate

Both platforms have blocking and reporting tools, the reported person is not notified, and there is no severity threshold to reach: content or a message that makes them uncomfortable is enough. Pass that on as is.

And recall the 4 warning signs [from the previous article](/en/articles/youth-online-5-good-practices) — insistent questions, photo requests, moving to private, requests for secrecy — with the escalation that goes with them: if an adult asks for photos or harassment gets organized, tell a trusted adult immediately, messages kept as evidence. Free helplines exist for young people and parents (France's 3018, Spain's 017, Bulgaria's safenet.bg — check your country's equivalent).

## The bonus worksite: the official family tools

Both platforms offer parent tools that almost nobody uses — and they beat any third-party monitoring app, because they're built in and transparent to the young person:

- **TikTok — Family Pairing**: links the parent's account to the young person's to manage screen time, messaging and content restrictions together.
- **Snapchat — Family Center**: shows the parent who the young person is exchanging with (never the content of the messages) and enables reporting.

Their strength is precisely their limit: they're installed *with* the young person's agreement, which makes them a tool for dialogue rather than hidden surveillance — exactly the spirit of this track.

## What really matters

**1. The account's age first.** All the default protections flow from it. A correctly dated account does 80% of the work.

**2. Ghost Mode and notifications off.** The two highest-yield settings: one closes the permanent location leak, the other breaks the platform's ability to summon the young person whenever it wants.

**3. The negotiated frame, not the imposed one.** Screen time, family tools, rules: what's discussed and understood holds when you're not there.

---

## A simple method to put in place

**This week:** check the declared age of the accounts together, then the worksite-1 settings pass on both platforms. Ghost Mode on Snap Map. Push notifications off.

**This month:** set the screen-time frame together (60 min on TikTok as the anchor), and propose the family tools — explaining what they show and what they don't.

**Ongoing habit:** the 20-second scan before every post (background, uniform, tag, caption), and a regular relaxed check-in about what they watch and post — conversation, not control.

**What "good enough" looks like:**

- **Beginner** — account ages verified, accounts private, Ghost Mode on, notifications off
- **Intermediate** — time limit negotiated and holding, 20-second scan a reflex, settings reviewed every few months
- **Advanced** — family tools active and accepted, the young person recognizes retention mechanics on their own, and helps friends with their settings

---

## An honest note

TikTok and Snapchat are not neutral tools: they are products whose revenue depends on captured attention. That doesn't make them bad — they can be genuinely creative, funny and social. But using them well requires conscious decisions that run against the platform's design.

These worksites won't make usage effortless. They'll make it intentional — and intentional use is markedly more enjoyable than the alternative, for the young person and for you.
