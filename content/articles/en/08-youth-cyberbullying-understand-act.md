# Cyberbullying: Detect, React, Make It Stop — A Guide for Parents and Educators

## Learning objective

After reading this article, you will be able to recognize the signs that a young person is being bullied online, react in the order that protects (and avoid the two reflexes that make things worse), mobilize the remedies that actually make it stop — and handle the case nobody talks about: when your child is the one doing the bullying.

---

## Understand first: why it differs from a schoolyard quarrel

Bullying among young people has always existed. Its online version changes four things, and understanding them shapes the whole response:

- **There is no respite anymore.** School bullying stopped at the gate; this follows the young person into their bedroom, at night, on weekends. Home stops being a refuge.
- **The audience is multiplied.** A humiliation in front of ten witnesses becomes a post visible to hundreds — and the uncertainty ("who has seen it?") is part of the wound.
- **It's permanent.** Screenshots, reposts: the content can resurface weeks later.
- **Anonymity disinhibits.** Fake accounts and pseudonyms get people to write what they would never say to someone's face — sometimes young people the victim knows well.

The concrete forms: insulting or threatening messages, comment-section pile-ons, organized exclusion from groups, spreading rumors or private content (photos, confidences), fake accounts created to impersonate the victim, mocking reposts of their content.

---

## Detect: the signs that should alert you

A bullied young person rarely says so on their own — out of shame, fear of retaliation, or fear of having their phone taken away. So it's the behavioral changes that speak:

- **Mood changes tied to the phone**: anxiety or dejection right after checking the screen, startling at notifications
- **Altered relationship with the phone, in either direction**: sudden abandonment of once-omnipresent devices, or compulsive, anxious checking
- **Social withdrawal**: avoiding friends and activities, refusal or dread of going to school or club
- **Diffuse signs**: disturbed sleep, changed appetite, unusual irritability, falling grades
- **New secrecy**: screen hidden when you approach, accounts suddenly deleted

None of these signs proves bullying on its own. Their accumulation, or their sudden appearance, justifies opening the conversation — calmly, without interrogation: *"You've seemed down lately, especially after being on your phone. Is something happening online?"*

## React: the order that protects

### Step 1 — The first words matter more than anything

If a young person talks to you — or if you discover the situation — four messages to deliver, in this order: **"You did the right thing telling me. This is not your fault. You won't be punished, and we won't take your phone away. We'll sort this out together."**

The third is decisive, and it's the [golden rule of this track](/en/articles/youth-online-5-good-practices): confiscating a bullied young person's phone is the **double punishment** — they suffer, then they're cut off from their social life. It's also the guarantee they'll say nothing next time — to you, or to the classmates who will hear about it.

### Step 2 — Document before blocking

The natural reflex is to delete everything and turn the page. Resist it long enough to **capture**: messages, posts, the profiles of the accounts involved, dates. This evidence conditions everything else — reports to the platform, to the school, a possible police report. Only then: **block** the accounts, and adjust settings to limit contact ([the platform-by-platform settings](/en/articles/tiktok-snapchat-good-practices)).

### Step 3 — Make it stop: the three remedies

- **The platform**: report the content and the accounts (the reported person is not notified). Useful, but often slow on its own.
- **A youth helpline**: most countries run free, confidential services that advise young people and parents, and can help get content taken down — France's **3018**, Spain's **017** (INCIBE), Bulgaria's **safenet.bg**; check your country's equivalent. This is the central resource at your disposal.
- **The school**, if classmates are involved — and they almost always are: online bullying extends a classroom dynamic. Schools have anti-bullying obligations and procedures; ask for them to be activated. And for serious situations — threats, sharing of intimate images, incitement — a police report is legitimate, evidence in hand: in many countries these behaviors are criminal offenses.

### Step 4 — Repair

Making it stop isn't always enough. If the young person remains marked — persistent withdrawal, anxiety, very dark talk — support from a professional (doctor, psychologist) is not a dramatic escalation: it's the normal care of a real wound.

## The two reflexes that make things worse

**Minimizing** — "it's only online, just turn off your phone". For a teenager, online life IS social life; that message teaches them you can't understand — and therefore can't help.

**Reacting in the heat of the moment on their behalf** — contacting the bully or their parents yourself in anger, posting a public response, demanding names on the spot. These reactions almost always escalate, expose your child to retaliation, and confirm to them that confiding unleashes storms. The anger is legitimate; it gets channeled into the documentation and the remedies of step 3.

And the message to pass on to the young person: **never retaliate online.** Retaliation feeds the fire, exposes itself to screenshots, and muddies responsibilities on the day the evidence matters.

---

## The case nobody talks about: if your child is the one bullying

It happens — often through group dynamics, without measuring the impact, sometimes in response to bullying suffered elsewhere. If the school or other parents alert you: neither excuse ("kids will be kids"), nor destroy (public humiliation teaches nothing).

The line: take the facts seriously, get the content removed, demand a complete stop, and look for the why — the group dynamic, what your child is fleeing or compensating for. An apology to the victim — sincere, not forced in public — is part of the repair. It's a hard conversation; it's also one of the most formative you'll have with them.

---

## What really matters

**1. The open door before the incident.** This whole track leads there: a young person who knows they'll be neither punished nor cut off from their phone talks early — and early, everything is easier to stop.

**2. Document, then block, then report — in that order.** Evidence first: it makes the difference between "word against word" and a situation that gets resolved.

**3. Your country's helpline saved in your phone.** Advice for parents, help with content takedowns, guidance: it's the central remedy, and it's free.

---

## A simple method to put in place

**This week:** state the "you can tell me, you won't be punished" guarantee if it isn't in place yet — it matters for bullying more than for anything else. Save your country's helpline in your contacts.

**This month:** a conversation about bullying — centered not on "is this happening to you" but on "do you see it around you": young people talk more easily as witnesses, and it's often the doorway to the rest.

**Ongoing habit:** attention to the signs from the start of this article — without surveillance or phone searches, which would destroy the trust everything depends on.

**What "good enough" looks like:**

- **Beginner** — the "no punishment" guarantee is in place, the helpline is known, and you know how to capture evidence on a phone
- **Intermediate** — the warning signs are known, the document → block → report order too, and the young person knows you never retaliate online
- **Advanced** — the young person comes to tell you what they see around them, intervenes healthily as a witness (reporting, private support — [article 5](/en/articles/social-media-responsible-use)), and the school knows you as a parent who handles these matters calmly

---

## An honest note

Discovering your child is being bullied — or bullying — is a shock, and nobody reacts perfectly under shock. If you've already had the wrong reflexes (minimizing, confiscating, exploding), nothing is set in stone: saying "I handled that badly, let's start over differently" is exactly the kind of sentence that reopens the door.

And you don't have to carry this alone: helplines advise parents as much as young people, schools have obligations, and professionals exist for what comes after. Bullying is fought as a network — that's precisely what makes it give way.
