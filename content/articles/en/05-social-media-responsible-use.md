# Using Social Media Responsibly: 5 Practices That Change Everything

## Learning objective

After reading this article, you will be able to apply five concrete practices for a safer, more thoughtful use of social media: protecting what you expose, verifying what you share, refusing to feed harassment, keeping control of your settings, and writing with impact in mind.

---

## A situation you might recognize

You're scrolling your feed when a shocking post appears — a claim about a public figure, a dramatic statistic, something that makes you angry. Your first reflex is to share. It feels important. It feels true.

Two hours later, another post: the original claim was false. Misleading. Taken out of context. But in the meantime, you've shared it — and some of your followers have shared it in turn.

This moment happens every day to careful, thoughtful people. Social media is built for speed, and speed makes us act before we think. The five practices in this article are designed to slow that mechanism down — in a lasting, realistic way.

---

## The 5 practices

### Practice 1 — Apply the stranger filter before posting

Full name, date of birth, phone number, school or employer, current location: each piece of information feels natural to share in a social context. On a public or semi-public platform, it travels far beyond your circle — and details harmless in isolation combine into a precise portrait. Your name, your neighborhood and your routine are enough to locate you.

**The filter:** before any personal post, one question — *would I be comfortable if a complete stranger had this information?* Address, phone number and daily schedule never appear publicly. For the full inventory of what you already expose and the 15-minute audit, see [reduce your online exposure](/en/articles/protect-your-private-life-online).

---

### Practice 2 — Verify before you share

Misinformation spreads faster than its corrections: a false claim shared once can reach thousands of people before any debunk exists. Every unverified share puts you in that chain — whatever your intentions.

**Why it happens:** platforms reward speed, and emotionally charged content triggers the urge to act right now. That is exactly what misinformation exploits: the more a post outrages you, the more it deserves verification.

**The practice — 60 seconds before sharing:** *where does this come from, and who has verified it?* Trace it to the original source. Compare with an established outlet (AFP, Reuters, AP). For viral claims, fact-checking services — **Snopes**, **FactCheck.org**, **Reuters Fact Check** — tell you in seconds whether it's already been debunked. The final rule is simple: **what you cannot confirm, you do not share.**

---

### Practice 3 — Neither the mob, nor the bystander

Cyberbullying takes many forms: direct harassment, comment-section pile-ons, reposting someone's content to mock them — and passive participation, by liking or laughing at posts designed to humiliate. Each of these actions causes real harm, even when it feels minor or anonymous in the moment.

**Why it happens:** distance reduces the sense of consequence, group dynamics make it easy to follow along, and doing nothing feels neutral — it isn't.

**The practice:** if you're targeted, document it (screenshots) and use the platform's reporting tools — you don't have to engage with the person. Many countries run dedicated helplines (France's 3018, Spain's 017, Bulgaria's safenet.bg). If you witness harassment, report the content and consider a private message of support to the person targeted — "I saw what happened and it wasn't okay" genuinely changes something. And never join a pile-on, even when the target seems to "deserve it": collective harassment escalates fast and strikes disproportionately. A full article in this track is dedicated to youth cyberbullying.

---

### Practice 4 — Keep control of your settings

Default settings maximize your visibility — good for the platform's growth, not necessarily for you. And they change with updates: a configuration done when you created the account is worthless two years later.

**The practice:** a quarterly review of the platforms you actively use — who sees your posts, who can message you, whether location is shared, which third-party apps have access. The two highest-yield moves: **switching to a private account** on Instagram and TikTok, and running Facebook's **Privacy Checkup** (a few guided minutes). The technical side — permissions, third-party logins — is covered in [reduce your exposure](/en/articles/protect-your-private-life-online).

---

### Practice 5 — Write with impact in mind

A comment written in anger, a joke that humiliates without meaning to, a tone that lands wrong: online communication removes the cues that calibrate our words in person — voice, face, body language. What feels light in your head can be received as aggression in text. And what's published is permanent: even deleted, someone may already have a screenshot.

**The practice:** before posting anything that involves another person, reread your message *as if you were receiving it*. The question: *does this contribute something, or am I just reacting?* And for any heated reply — conflict, negative comment — give yourself **ten minutes** before hitting send. Strong emotions produce messages that almost always benefit from a cooling-off period.

---

## What really matters

**1. Verify before sharing.** Not relaying what you can't confirm: this single habit, held over time, makes you a healthy link in the information chain.

**2. Review your settings every three months.** Platforms change, defaults change, your own comfort level changes. A brief regular review guarantees you're sharing what you intend to share — nothing more.

**3. The pause before sending.** 60 seconds for a piece of information, 10 minutes for a heated reply: the simplest and most effective filter at your disposal.

---

## A simple method to put in place

**This week:** review the settings of your two most-used networks. Private account where it isn't yet, check of who can message you and of location sharing.

**This month:** at the next surprising piece of information, practice the 60 seconds: original source, established outlet, fact-checker. Share only if confirmed.

**Ongoing habit:** reread every contentious message as if you were the recipient. If you wouldn't want to receive it, rewrite it — or don't send it.

**What "good enough" looks like:**

- **Beginner** — main profiles private, no address or phone number public, reporting tool used at least once
- **Intermediate** — systematic verification before sharing surprising content, quarterly settings review, pause respected before heated replies
- **Advanced** — you help others navigate misinformation, you understand what the platforms collect, and the way you write online is the way you'd stand behind face to face

---

## An honest note

Building good social media habits is genuinely hard — these platforms are specifically designed to encourage fast, emotional engagement. The practices in this article run against their architecture.

Mistakes are therefore inevitable. One day you'll share something that turns out false; you'll post a frustrated message you regret. The goal isn't perfection: it's a general trend toward more thought and more intention. Every small improvement compounds.
