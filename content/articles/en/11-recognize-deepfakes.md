# Recognizing Deepfakes: Why Your Eyes Aren't Enough — and What Works Instead

## Learning objective

After reading this article, you will understand what deepfakes are and why they've become trivial to produce, you will know the three scam patterns they power, and you will have context-based verification reflexes that work regardless of how perfect the fake looks: because that is the part the technology cannot fake.

---

## A situation to know about

Your phone rings. It's your son's voice (unmistakably his) panicked: he's had an accident abroad, his phone is broken, he's calling from a borrowed number, and he needs money wired in the next hour. The voice is right. The emotion is right. Everything in you wants to act immediately.

Except it isn't him. A few seconds of audio (from a voicemail greeting, a social media video) is enough today to clone a voice convincingly. This scam, in countless variants, is no longer science fiction: it runs daily, against families and against companies.

## What a deepfake is — and why it's now easy

A deepfake is synthetic media (image, video or audio) generated or altered by AI to show a real person doing or saying something they never did or said. The same family of technology that generates plausible text ([see under the hood](/en/articles/ai-advanced-notions)) generates plausible pixels and plausible sound.

Three things changed in recent years, and they define the threat:

- **Quality:** the obvious glitches of early deepfakes have largely disappeared from competent fakes.
- **Accessibility:** producing one no longer requires skills or budget, consumer tools do it in minutes.
- **Raw material:** a few photos and seconds of audio suffice, and most of us have published years' worth of both.

---

## The central rule: your eyes are no longer the test

You may have read lists of visual tells: malformed hands, odd blinking, blurry hair edges, mismatched lighting. Here is the honest status of that advice: **when such artifacts are present, they do prove manipulation — but their absence proves nothing.** Generation quality improves continuously, and the fakes that matter (targeted, prepared) are precisely the ones without obvious flaws. Building your safety on visual inspection means building on ground that erodes every month.

What the technology cannot fake is the **context** around the content:

- **The source**: who published this, on what channel, and is that the person's or organization's known account?
- **The channel**: why is this reaching you here (unknown number, new account, forwarded message) rather than through the normal channel?
- **The pressure**: why must you act *right now*, without checking?
- **The request** — money, credentials, intimate images, a click: is there something to extract from you?

These four questions work on a perfect deepfake exactly as well as on a crude one. That is why they're the method.

## The three scam patterns deepfakes power

### Pattern 1 — The cloned voice of a loved one

The scenario from the introduction: a family member in distress, an urgent transfer, a plausible reason why they're unreachable on their usual number. Sometimes a video call "that keeps cutting out": long enough to show a face, short enough to hide flaws.

**The counter:** hang up and **call back on the number you already have** for that person. Not the number that called you: your own contact entry. Ninety seconds of "inconvenience" against your savings. And set up a **family code word** in advance: a word or question only your family knows, agreed offline, asked whenever an emergency call requests money. A cloned voice has the timbre; it doesn't have the code.

### Pattern 2 — The fake authority

A "manager" ordering an urgent transfer, a "bank advisor" video-calling about fraud on your account, an "official" demanding information. Corporate variants have cost companies millions; individual variants target everyone.

**The counter:** the same as [for phishing](/en/articles/recognize-a-phishing-email), because it's the same psychology with better props: **no legitimate organization demands instant action on an unusual channel.** Verify through the channel you already know: the official number, the branch, the internal procedure. In a company: any payment instruction received by call or video gets confirmed through a second, independent channel, whoever appears to be asking.

### Pattern 3 — The viral shock content

A public figure saying something outrageous, a dramatic scene from a conflict, "leaked footage": designed to be shared in anger. Deepfakes have become a standard tool of disinformation, and [the rule from the social media track](/en/articles/social-media-responsible-use) applies doubled: **the more a video outrages you, the more it deserves verification.**

**The counter:** look for the original source and the earliest publication. A reverse image search (Google Lens, TinEye) on a key frame often surfaces the original context, or the debunk. Established fact-checkers now treat viral synthetic media as core business; check them before sharing. What you cannot confirm, you do not share.

## Protecting yourself upstream — and reacting if targeted

**Limit the raw material.** Cloning needs samples: public videos of your voice, high-resolution photos from every angle. You don't need to disappear, but [the exposure logic](/en/articles/protect-your-private-life-online) applies: public voice notes, public video archives and open profiles are the feedstock. For children, this is one more reason [sharenting deserves restraint](/en/articles/youth-online-5-good-practices).

**If you or someone close is targeted**, including intimate-image deepfakes, which increasingly target ordinary people and minors: **do not pay, do not negotiate, and do not delete anything.** Capture the evidence, report to the platform (non-consensual intimate imagery is banned everywhere and prioritized for removal), and report to the authorities, in many countries, creating or sharing such content is now a specific criminal offense. Helplines can guide you: France's 3018 (for minors) and cybermalveillance.gouv.fr, Spain's 017, Bulgaria's safenet.bg, the UK's Revenge Porn Helpline, the US FBI at ic3.gov. A victim has done nothing wrong: the shame belongs entirely to the maker.

## What really matters

**1. Absence of flaws proves nothing.** Judge content by its context (source, channel, pressure, request) not by its pixels. The four questions work on perfect fakes.

**2. Urgency + money + unusual channel = fraud until proven otherwise.** One rule covers cloned voices, fake managers and fake advisors alike. Hang up, call back on the known number.

**3. Agree on the family code word this week.** Two minutes around the dinner table, and the most cruel scam in circulation loses its power over your family.

---

## A simple method to put in place

**This week:** set the family code word, with your partner, children, parents. Explain why in one sentence: "if anyone calls in an emergency asking for money, we ask for the word."

**This month:** practice one reverse image search on any viral image, just to have the reflex ready. And review your public voice/video exposure ([the 15-minute audit](/en/articles/protect-your-private-life-online)).

**Ongoing habit:** for any urgent request involving money or sensitive data (whatever the voice or face attached) verification through an independent, known channel before any action. No exceptions, especially not for emotion: emotion is the payload.

**What "good enough" looks like:**

- **Beginner**: you know that visual inspection isn't a test, the family code word exists, and you never act on an urgent call without calling back on a known number
- **Intermediate**: the four context questions are a reflex, you can run a reverse image search, and you check fact-checkers before sharing shock content
- **Advanced**: your family and colleagues know the counter-measures because you've briefed them, and payment instructions at work always get second-channel confirmation

## An honest note

This article will age in one direction only: the fakes will get better, and the tells will get rarer. That is precisely why it teaches context, not pixels: the four questions will work in five years exactly as they do today.

And keep the base rate in mind: most of what you see remains authentic. The goal isn't paranoia toward every video: it's a trained reflex for the specific moment when urgency, money and an unusual channel line up. That combination, human or synthetic, has always deserved your suspicion.
